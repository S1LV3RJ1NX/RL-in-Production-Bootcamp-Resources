On a laptop, our first project taught a small model to fix bugs in about half an hour. That was the friendly, everything-fits-on-your-desk version. But real training — bigger models, real programming problems, hours or days of work — outgrows a laptop fast, and the moment it does, a second, sneakier kind of difficulty shows up. It is not about the math. It is about keeping a very long job *alive*. This chapter is Project Two: the same learning loop you already understand, run for real on rented machines in the cloud — and the honest, modest result it produced.

## Why a laptop is not enough anymore

In [the last project](training-on-a-laptop.html) the model was small — **1.5 billion** tunable numbers inside it — and the puzzles were tiny hand-written functions. That is exactly why it fit on a MacBook. Project Two steps up on both counts. We want to try bigger models, and we want to train on **MBPP+** (`evalplus/mbppplus`), a set of **378** real small Python problems, each phrased like a request a person might actually make: *"write a function to…"*. To train on that at any reasonable speed, a laptop simply is not fast enough.

The bottleneck is a specific chip. When a model "thinks," it does an enormous pile of multiplication all at once, and there is a chip built for exactly that. A **GPU** (graphics processing unit — the specialized chip that makes model training fast) does thousands of those multiplications in parallel, where an ordinary laptop processor does a few at a time. [[sn: GPUs were invented to draw video-game graphics, which also means doing huge numbers of small multiplications at once. It turned out that "draw a 3D scene" and "run a neural network" are, deep down, the same kind of arithmetic — so the gaming chip became the AI chip.]] The one we used is an **H100**, a high-end data-center GPU — the kind of chip you do not buy for your desk, you *rent* for the hour.

And renting is exactly what we did, through a service called **Modal** (a cloud service that rents you GPUs and runs your code on them). You write your training program on your laptop as usual, then hand it to Modal, which runs it on a real H100 somewhere in a data center and streams the results back to you. No hardware to buy, no cables to plug in — you borrow a powerful machine for as long as the job takes.

## The exact same loop, only bigger

Here is the reassuring part. Nothing about *how* the model learns changed. We ran the very same **GRPO** ([Group Relative Policy Optimization](grpo-learning-from-a-group.html)) loop from Project One: for one problem, let the model try several times, score each attempt by running its tests, then nudge the model toward whatever scored above the group's average. Try, score, nudge — unchanged. [[note: aha || Moving to the cloud does not change the idea of learning; it changes the *plumbing*. The loop that took a laptop from 66.7% to 73.3% is the identical loop running here. All that grew is the machine underneath it.]]

The reward is the same simple thing, too. Each MBPP+ problem comes with a handful of **visible tests** — the checks the model is allowed to see and is scored on — and a larger hidden set the model never trains on. The reward is just: did the visible tests pass? `reward = 1.0` if they all pass, `0.0` if not. [[sn: The hidden tests are the honest ground truth. If a model passes the few visible tests but fails the hidden ones, it did not really solve the problem — it only satisfied the specific checks it could see. We keep the hidden tests locked away precisely so they can tell us the truth afterward.]]

We ran this across **Qwen2.5-Coder** models in three sizes — **0.5B**, **1.5B**, and **7B**. Fitting the largest one onto a single GPU took one more trick called **LoRA** (a lightweight fine-tuning method that trains a small set of add-on numbers instead of all seven billion), which is what lets a 7B model train on one rented H100 without needing a whole cluster.

## Reading the honest learning curve

For the story we tell, we deliberately featured the *smallest* model, the **0.5B**. That sounds backwards — why show off the weakest one? Because it starts with the most room to grow. The 7B model already solved about **84%** of the tasks *before any training at all*; it was near the ceiling, with almost nothing left to demonstrate.

[[note: confusion || A beginner reasonably assumes the biggest model makes the best demo. For *showing learning*, the opposite is true: a model that already aces everything has no headroom, so training barely moves its score. The small model, starting low, has somewhere to climb — which makes the before-and-after visible.]] The little 0.5B model started low, so any real learning would actually show.

And it did. On a held-out set of tasks it had never trained on, the 0.5B model went from solving **44/100** to **51/100**. Watch the shape of the climb, because the shape is the honest part: the curve **rises sharply** in the first steps — from a solve fraction of **0.44** up to roughly **0.55–0.61** — and then it **plateaus**. It learns fast, then holds. The whole run was **80 steps** on an H100.

Let me be plain about what this is, because the honesty is the point. This is not a dramatic leap. It is a real but modest improvement. The cleanest way to say what happened is this: **of the tasks the model was failing, RL taught it to solve 14.** Fourteen problems flipped from fail to pass — genuine fixes, not lucky guesses. That is a true result, and reporting it exactly, without inflation, is more valuable than a shiny headline number that would not survive a careful reader's checking.

[[sn: One quiet caveat, saved for the final chapter: because the reward only watches the *visible* tests, a clever enough model can sometimes learn to satisfy those specific checks without truly solving the problem — a mild way of gaming the reward. There is a fix, and we will get to it.]]

## The lesson that has nothing to do with math

Now the part I most want a beginner to take away, because nobody warns you about it. The hardest bug in Project Two was not in the model. It was in *keeping the training job alive*.

Here is the trap. When you run a program from your laptop against the cloud in the naive way, the job is tethered to your laptop's session — the live connection between your machine and the rented one. Close the lid, lose your WiFi for a moment, or let the session time out, and the cloud dutifully **kills the job**. Hours of a long RL run, gone, just because your laptop went to sleep.

A red lightning bolt cutting the blue line labeled "laptop sleeps / WiFi drops", and a big red cross on the cloud box with the handwritten note "job KILLED". A green note: "laptop can close — job keeps running". Blue arrow between the panels labeled "deploy to the cloud". Dashed takeaway box in black: "detach the job from your machine". || The systems lesson: a tethered job dies when your laptop does; a deployed job runs fully server-side and survives.]]

The fix is a shift in thinking, not a cleverer algorithm. Instead of *running* the job from your laptop, you **deploy** it — you hand the whole thing to the cloud so it runs entirely server-side, on Modal's own machines, with no dependence on your laptop at all. Now the job is **durable** (it keeps running no matter what your computer does). You can shut your laptop, walk away, come back in an hour, and reconnect to a job that never stopped. [[note: production || Every serious training run works this way. A real RL run can last hours or days, and over that span *something* will go wrong on your end — a dropped connection, a closed lid, a restart. Grown-up systems assume it and detach the job from the human's machine entirely. The training must survive the mess of the real world, or it never finishes.]]

That is the real headline of this project, quieter than any accuracy number: **training real models is a systems problem, not just a math problem.** The GRPO loop was already correct — we ported it straight from the laptop. What made Project Two hard was making a long-running job survive network drops, timeouts, and restarts. Getting the learning *idea* right is maybe half the battle; getting the machinery to run reliably for hours is the other half, and it is the half the textbooks skip.

If you would like to see the fourteen flipped tasks for yourself, the beginner site at `rl-teaches-code.vercel.app` shows these exact before-and-after fixes live; a deeper research write-up lives separately at `swe-rl-ipr.vercel.app` for the curious.

## Where this leaves us

Project Two kept the learning loop identical and changed only the world it runs in — a rented GPU, a real benchmark, and a job robust enough to survive the internet. The payoff was honest: a small model, taught to solve fourteen problems it used to fail. But every model here still learns by *acting* — it writes a fix, gets scored, and moves on, with no sense of what its actions will *do* before it takes them. The next and most advanced project asks a deeper question: what if the agent could also learn to *predict* its world — to know, before it flips the switch, that the light will come on? That is ECHO, and it is where we go next.

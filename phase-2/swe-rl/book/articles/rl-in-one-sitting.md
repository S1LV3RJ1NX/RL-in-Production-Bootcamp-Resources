This whole book rests on one idea, and it is simpler than it sounds: you can teach a machine the way you teach a child to ride a bike — not by writing down the answer, but by letting it try, telling it whether the try worked, and letting it lean into whatever worked. That single loop, drawn out and made precise, is **reinforcement learning**, and it is the engine behind all three projects in this book. Let us build it from scratch, one plain sentence at a time.

Here is that sentence, and we will unpack it for the rest of the chapter: **try something, see if it worked, do more of what worked.** That is the whole of it. Everything else — the code, the math, the GPUs — is detail hung on those nine words.

## Learning by trying, not by copying

Think about how you learned to ride a bike. Nobody handed you a written manual of exact muscle movements. You climbed on, wobbled, fell, and the *world itself* told you how you did — a scrape meant "not that," a smooth glide meant "yes, more of that." Over many tries, you drifted toward the glides. No teacher wrote down the correct answer; the falling and the gliding *were* the teacher.

[[note: metaphor || A dog learning a trick is the same story. You do not explain "sit" in English — you say the word, wait, and the instant the dog's back end touches the floor, a treat appears. The treat is a signal, not an instruction. After enough treats the dog has *figured out* what earns them. It learned by trying and being scored, never by being shown the answer.]]

This is worth pausing on, because it is the opposite of how machines are *usually* taught. The common way to train a **language model** (a computer program that reads text and writes text back, one word at a time) is to show it millions of examples written by humans — this question, that correct answer — and have it copy the pattern. [[sn: This copy-the-humans method has a proper name, *supervised learning*, and it is how most of a model's abilities are first built. Reinforcement learning usually comes *after*, to sharpen a skill the model already half-has. The two are partners, not rivals.]] That works beautifully when a human already knows the right answer and can write it down. But for many jobs, nobody has written down the answer — and writing one down for every case would take forever. Reinforcement learning sidesteps that entirely: there is **no labeled right answer**. The learner just tries, and something in the world scores the attempt.

## The loop, named precisely

Let us now name the parts, because we will use these four words for the rest of the book. The learner is the **agent** (the thing doing the trying — in our case, the language model). The thing it does is an **action** (here, writing a fix for a piece of buggy code). The world it acts on is the **environment** (the code, plus the machinery that runs it). And after each action, the environment hands back a **reward** — a single number where high means good — along with a new situation to face. The agent's whole goal is to earn more reward over time.

[[fig: The RL loop drawn as a clean cycle of four rounded cards joined by curved blue arrows, titled "the loop" in black. Top card (black) "AGENT" with a small model icon and the blue label "tries something". Blue arrow curving right, labeled "ACTION — writes a fix" in blue, to the right card (black) "ENVIRONMENT" drawn as a little machine with gears, labeled "runs it". From ENVIRONMENT a blue arrow curves down to the bottom card labeled "REWARD" with a big green "1 = good / 0 = bad" inside it. A blue arrow curves left and up, labeled "LEARN — nudge toward what worked" in blue, back to AGENT. A numbered circle "1" sits on AGENT, "2" on the action arrow, "3" on REWARD, "4" on the learn arrow. Dashed takeaway box in black at bottom: "try -> score -> nudge, forever". || The reinforcement learning loop: an agent acts, the environment returns a reward and a new situation, and the agent nudges itself toward whatever earned reward. Read the circles 1 to 4 and round again.]]

Round and round it goes. The agent acts; the environment scores; the agent adjusts; the agent acts again, a little wiser. There is no step where a human leans in and says "the answer was X." The only thing flowing back is that number, the reward, and everything the agent ever learns is squeezed out of it. [[sn: The formal name for the agent's decision-making — its current strategy for choosing actions — is the **policy**. When we say the model "learns," we mean its policy shifts a little after each round. In this book the policy simply *is* the language model and its billions of tunable numbers.]]

## What makes a good reward

The reward is the heart of it, and choosing a good one is the whole art. It has to be a number the environment can produce *by itself*, quickly, with no argument. That rules out a lot of jobs. "Write a beautiful poem" has no reward button — beauty is a matter of taste, and there is no gadget that returns *correct* or *incorrect*. Ask ten people and you get ten opinions.

Code is gloriously different. And this is where our earlier chapter on [what a software task is](what-is-a-swe-task.html) pays off. Recall that a software engineering task comes with a **test** — a tiny second piece of code that checks the first one, like `assert add(2, 2) == 4`, and either **passes** or **fails** with no room for opinion. That gives us the perfect reward: run the tests, and hand back `reward = 1.0` if every test passes and `reward = 0.0` if any test fails. Binary. Automatic. Unarguable.

[[note: aha || Here is the sentence to carry through the whole book: **the tests are the teacher.** We never sit and hand-label the correct code for the machine. We hand it a broken function, let it try a fix, and let the tests say pass or fail. That `1.0` or `0.0` is the entire teaching signal — free, instant, and honest.]]

## A tiny turn of the loop, by hand

Let us walk one turn, slowly, with a real bug from the first project. There is a function meant to compute the volume of a triangular prism. The model's first attempt writes `return length * width * height` — but that is the volume of a *box*, not a prism, which is only *half* a box. The action is written. The environment runs the tests. They fail. The reward comes back `0.0`.

[[note: example || Turn one: the model writes `length * width * height`, the tests fail, `reward = 0.0`. The loop nudges it *away* from that. Turn two, a little later, it tries `0.5 * base * height * width` — the missing one-half restored. The tests pass, `reward = 1.0`. The loop nudges it *toward* that. Two numbers, `0.0` and `1.0`, and the model has quietly learned that a prism is half a box.]]

That is the loop in miniature: a wrong try scored `0.0` and pushed away, a right try scored `1.0` and pulled toward. Do this thousands of times, across dozens of bugs, and a model that was guessing starts, measurably, to fix. No manual, no answer key — just trying and scoring, over and over.

## One loop, three projects

The remarkable thing is how little this changes as the projects grow. The same four-part loop — agent, action, reward, environment — drives everything ahead of us, only the *size* of each part changes.

[[fig: A "scale map" of three stacked rows under the title "one loop, three sizes" in black, each row a horizontal strip with the same four small icons (agent, action, environment, green reward tag) but growing. Row 1 (black label "PROJECT 1 — puzzles on a laptop"): tiny model icon, "fix a function", a small gear box, green tag "1 / 0". Row 2 (black label "PROJECT 2 — real Python tasks on a cloud GPU"): bigger model, "write a whole function", a stack of files, green tag "1 / 0". Row 3 (black label "PROJECT 3 — a terminal agent"): biggest model, "type many commands", a dark terminal window, green tag "1 / 0". A tall orange bracket down the left margin labeled "SAME LOOP" spans all three rows. Dashed takeaway box: "the agent, action, reward, environment never change — only how big they get". || The same loop scaled three ways: fixing one function on a laptop, solving real Python tasks on a rented GPU, and driving a terminal step by step. The parts are identical; only their size grows.]]

In the first project, a small model fixes buggy Python functions on a **MacBook**, and the reward is whether a handful of tests pass. In the second, a model tackles real programming tasks on a rented cloud **GPU** (a specialized chip that makes model training fast), with the same pass/fail reward. In the third, the agent solves problems by typing shell commands in a **terminal** (a text window where you type commands to the computer and it types back), one step after another, still scored by whether the job got done. Different worlds, different scales — one loop.

And the recipe that turns those scored attempts into an actual improvement to the model has a name we will meet next: **GRPO**, short for Group Relative Policy Optimization. It is the exact algorithm behind the best open coding agents, and it is friendlier than its name suggests. That is where we go now.

The bike, the dog, the tests — all of it comes down to try, score, nudge. In the [next chapter](grpo-learning-from-a-group.html) we open up the "nudge" and see precisely how a group of tries teaches a model to do better.
